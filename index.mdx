## The business problem

An insurer needs to answer three questions every morning, from data that was correct **as of a specific moment**:

<CardGroup cols={3}>
  <Card title="Loss ratio" icon="chart-line">
    How much are we paying out compared to the premium we collected?
  </Card>
  <Card title="Reserving" icon="piggy-bank">
    For claims still open, how much money must we set aside?
  </Card>
  <Card title="Claims operations" icon="headset">
    What is happening right now — new claims, backlogs, one storm hitting many policies at once?
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

The awkward part is the phrase *as of a specific moment*. An auditor may ask what the reserve figure was on 31 March. If the reporting system only ever holds the latest values, that question cannot be answered. The history has to be captured as it happens.

That single requirement is what shapes the whole architecture.

## Why the data is hard to get

The numbers live in two separate operational systems that were never designed to be read together:

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Policy administration" icon="file-contract">
    Policyholders, policies, coverages, and endorsements (mid-term changes).
  </Card>
  <Card title="Claims" icon="folder-open">
    Claims, adjusters, status history, and financial transactions.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

They are two different databases owned by two different teams. A claim stores a `policy_number` as plain text — there is no database-level link between the two systems, because a foreign key cannot span two databases. Some claims therefore reference policies that do not exist. That is not a bug in this project; it is a deliberate reproduction of something that happens in every real insurer, and the pipeline has to detect and quarantine it rather than pretend it away.

<Note>
**This project is a data platform, not a machine-learning project.** There is no model, no prediction, no GenAI layer. The goal is a correct, auditable, incrementally-loaded warehouse — which is what the job market actually pays for.
</Note>

## What "correct" means here

Three properties the pipeline must hold, which come up constantly in design discussions:

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Point-in-time accuracy" icon="clock-rotate-left">
    You must be able to reconstruct the state of any claim on any past date. This is why changes are captured as a stream of events rather than a nightly snapshot of current values — a snapshot taken at midnight silently loses every change that happened and was reversed during the day.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Idempotency" icon="rotate">
    Running the same load twice must not double-count anything. Pipelines fail and get retried; if a retry inflates your paid-claims figure, the number is worthless.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Auditability" icon="magnifying-glass">
    For any number in a report, you must be able to trace it back to the source row and the moment it changed. "The dashboard says 64%" is not an answer an auditor accepts.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Where the build currently stands

<Steps>
  <Step title="Source databases" icon="check">
    Two Postgres instances running in Docker, configured for logical replication, with schemas created and change-capture enabled. **Done.**
  </Step>
  <Step title="Seed data" icon="check">
    Around 160,000 rows of realistic insurance data — policies, claims, reserves, payments, recoveries, and one shared catastrophe event. **Done.**
  </Step>
  <Step title="Kafka and Debezium" icon="hourglass">
    Stream changes out of Postgres into Kafka topics. **Next.**
  </Step>
  <Step title="S3 and Snowflake" icon="hourglass">
    Land events as Parquet, ingest into a raw layer, then build current-state tables.
  </Step>
  <Step title="dbt and orchestration" icon="hourglass">
    Transform into analytics-ready marts with tests, scheduled by Airflow.
  </Step>
</Steps>
